Dauvergne Notes Ch2 and Ch3

Chapter 2

  • The argument: For the developing world, globalization is imperialism continued under another name.
  • European imperialism serving as the root of modern globalization
  • Case study: Captain Quieros and the start of imperial domination in what is now Vanuatu
  • Globalization can be every bit as violent and exploitative as imperialism
    • e.g. world powers forcing developing countries to deregulate economic affairs, to open borders to foreign capital and trade
  • Current evidence: Massive and rising debt of the developing world; Rising inequality, concentrating financial power

Chapter 3

  • The Argument: Europeans brought capitalism into Nauru and once a valuable resource was discovered Nauru destroyed itself.
  • Europeans don’t stumble onto tiny Nauru until 1798
  • By accident, 1899-1901 Phosphate ore is discovered
  • By 1968, 1/3 of Naur strip-mined
  • After independence, Nauru chose to increase mining despite knowing supplies would shortly run out, exports generated much wealth.
  • Desperate to replace income, Nauru becomes haven for dirty banks, money laundering and starts selling off citizenships.
  • By the early 2000s supplies plummett and economic catastrophe is wrought.
  • At this point, Nauru has allowed Australia to set up a detention camp for would-be immigrants as it needs the money for hosting them.

This is data from UN Trade and Development (UNCTAD)

  • The rich world draws the developing world into global trade with the promise of development

  • HOWEVER, the risks they face are greater, so they are charged a premium “to play” in global markets


Since 2020, developing countries have been borrowing at average rates two to four times higher than those for the United States, making it harder to invest in sustainable development.

  • High interest rates, weak global growth and rising uncertainty are squeezing public budgets.

  • The consequences are direct and devastating, as people – especially vulnerable populations – pay the price.


For academics like Dauvergne, this suggests that globalization has not been designed primarily to benefit the developing world

  • It has been structured to continue our exploitation of it


SLIDE: What do these growing debt burdens mean for the developing world?

Public debt can be a powerful tool for development.

  • Governments use it to invest in their people and economies – and pave the way to a better future.

  • The promise of globalization was to improve standards of living everywhere, but…


But when debt grows too large or becomes too costly, it turns into a burden.

  • That is the current reality for much of the developing world.

  • Today, a total of 3.4 billion people now live in countries that spend more on debt interest than on either health or education.
